# Strategy

Voice makes a message sound like the user; these make it work. Read when the message is an ask, a decline, bad news, feedback, a delicate answer, or anything upward or external. Skip it for logistics, family, and banter. Apply the two or three principles the message needs, never all of them; if a rewrite stops sounding like the user, keep the structure and drop the polish.

Adapted from Wes Kao's frameworks via kinantid/skills (MIT); the warm no draws on Alison Green.

**Lead with the point.** The first sentence carries the ask, the headline, or the number; backstory gets one sentence or none. Test: delete the first paragraph; if the message still works, it was setup.

**Name the obvious objection.** What will a sharp reader push back on in ten seconds? Say it and answer it in the same breath, one or two objections at most. Never name one with a handwaved answer; if there is no one-sentence answer, the pitch is not ready.

**Make the yes easy.** Lead with the benefit to them, scope the work so the yes is approval not a project, bound the ask ("a review on <link>, ~50 lines"), and replace "ASAP" or "when you get a chance" with a date, a sequence, or a default ("if I don't hear back by 5pm I'll go with A"). Too big for an enthusiastic yes: ask for the direction, debate details after.

**The warm no.** The no stays clear; the warmth lives in the close. Carry the next step yourself ("I'll ping you when we pick this up"), offer only what you will honour, and add one true specific about them. Three specifics read as a CRM record; a garbled one is worse than none.

**Finesse a delicate answer.** Same facts, chosen framing. "500" is inert; "we don't have hard limits but you might hit issues at scale" plants a doubt in every clause; "it comfortably handles 500 concurrent users, well past your size, and we'll flag before you near any threshold" leads with the capability. Audit for "don't", "issues", "usually", "should be fine". Every reframe must stay true; otherwise it is spin.

**Invert the but.** Readers keep what follows "but". When the positive is the honest headline, write "[negative], but [positive]". Never invert a real risk flag or incident news.

**Receipts before you credit.** Every specific in a compliment needs a source. Don't credit work that happened differently, inflate scope, or tell someone to develop a skill they just showed. A label ("great leadership") is not an observation.

**Bad news: objective, not detached.** Lead with the fact and its size ("12 profiles, 3 noticed before we did"), one severity line, own it once ("my miss"), close on containment. Passive voice here is the tell. A reasonable decision someone won't love is not a mistake; state it with the reasoning and do not apologise.

**Speak in the affirmative.** "Keep blockers to two minutes, then priorities" beats "don't spend standup on blockers". "I can pick this up Thursday" beats "I can't until Thursday". When the negative is the message, follow it with the replacement.

**Answer the real question.** "What's the status?" usually means "will I be surprised, and do you need anything from me?" Answer that first and let the literal answer ride along; if the two diverge, name both.
